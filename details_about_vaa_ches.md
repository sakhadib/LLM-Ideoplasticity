Voting Advice Applications (VAAs) and the Chapel Hill Expert Survey (CHES) are now core infrastructures for studying and structuring the political space in Europe, especially around party competition and European integration. Together they offer complementary, citizen‑facing and expert‑driven lenses on European politics, and their interaction is central to contemporary research on party systems, cleavages, and democratic representation in the EU. [chesdata](https://www.chesdata.eu/ches-europe)

## Overview of VAAs  

A Voting Advice Application is an online tool that helps voters identify parties or candidates whose policy positions are closest to their own preferences. In a typical VAA, users answer a set of issue statements—often on a Likert‑type agreement scale—and the application computes proximity scores to parties’ positions on the same issues, presenting a ranked list or spatial visualization of party matches. [europeangovernanceandpolitics.eui](https://europeangovernanceandpolitics.eui.eu/project/research-and-impact-through-european-wide-and-national-voting-advice-applications-euandi/)

VAAs became widespread across Europe in the 2000s and have been deployed for national and European Parliament elections in a majority of European countries. Early and highly visible examples include the Dutch StemWijzer/ VoteMatch and the German Wahl‑O‑Mat, which reached millions of users and, in some elections, substantial shares of the electorate. This diffusion has turned VAAs into a distinct subfield of research within European political science, with dedicated networks and comparative projects. [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_advice_application)

## VAAs in European politics  

The European context is particularly conducive to the spread of VAAs because proportional representation systems, multi‑party competition, and complex coalition dynamics make party choice less obvious than in two‑party systems. Research has shown that VAAs tend to be used more intensively in countries with proportional electoral rules and larger numbers of parliamentary parties, such as Belgium, Finland, the Netherlands, and Switzerland. [ecpr](https://ecpr.eu/group/voting-advice-applications)

European Parliament elections have been an especially fertile ground for VAAs, often described as “second‑order” elections where voters are more willing to experiment or vote closer to their ideological preferences. Pan‑European VAAs for EP elections—such as EU Profiler (2009) and its successors—allow citizens to situate themselves not only in their domestic party space but across the entire EU party arena. This transnational dimension is normatively important in a multilevel polity like the EU, where party competition is simultaneously national and European. [euandi](https://euandi.eu/)

## EU&I / euandi as flagship VAA  

The EU&I (euandi) project hosted by the European University Institute (EUI) is the most prominent transnational VAA for EP elections. First launched as EU Profiler in 2009, it has been redeployed for subsequent European Parliament elections (2014, 2019, 2024), covering all EU member states and multiple non‑EU European countries. [euandi](https://euandi.eu/en/about/overview.html)

EU&I invites users to react to 30 policy statements that span contemporary political issues and values in European politics, using a five‑point scale from “completely agree” to “completely disagree”, plus a “no opinion” option. The 2024 version, available in over 20 languages, attracted roughly 850,000 users in 30 days, mapping positions for around 290 parties and a small number of independent candidates. The platform is explicitly designed to be politically neutral, developed by academics, and offered free of charge to citizens, researchers, and practitioners. [euandi](https://euandi.eu/sk/faq.html)

## VAA methodology and party placement  

A central methodological challenge for VAAs is determining parties’ positions on the issue statements used in the questionnaire. EU&I uses a hybrid method that combines party self‑placement with expert judgment: parties are invited to state their positions, and teams of country experts cross‑check and code positions where necessary. The statements themselves are selected through a careful, iterative process aimed at capturing salient and divisive issues in European politics while retaining cross‑national comparability. [academia](https://www.academia.edu/81175531/Chapel_Hill_Expert_Survey_trend_file_1999_2019)

The euandi trend file (2009–2019) illustrates the scale of this data collection: it compiles positions of 411 parties from 28 European countries on a wide range of salient political issues, yielding over 20,000 unique party‑issue placements across three EP elections. Fifteen statements remained identical across the 2009, 2014, and 2019 waves, enabling limited longitudinal analysis of party positions and issue cleavages. These design choices make EU&I not just a voter‑support tool, but also a major cross‑national dataset on party policy positions. [academia](https://www.academia.edu/81175531/Chapel_Hill_Expert_Survey_trend_file_1999_2019)

## VAAs as research data  

Because VAAs record both users’ responses and parties’ positions, they generate rich data for electoral, party, and representation research. The EU&I team explicitly emphasizes that their applications produce “highly relevant scientific data” which are regularly made available to the academic community, subject to privacy and ethical standards. [europeangovernanceandpolitics.eui](https://europeangovernanceandpolitics.eui.eu/project/research-and-impact-through-european-wide-and-national-voting-advice-applications-euandi/)

Researchers use VAA party data to study European party systems, ideological mapping, and issue competition, while user data can be exploited to analyze mass opinion distributions, patterns of issue alignment, and potential representation gaps. Comparative work on EP elections, using EU Profiler and EU&I data, has examined parties’ positions on foreign and security policy, European integration, and other salient issues across the EU‑28 in multiple electoral cycles. These analyses feed into broader debates about the nature of second‑order elections, transnational party competition, and the emergence of new cleavages in European politics. [academia](https://www.academia.edu/281395/Voting_Advice_Applications_in_Europe_The_State_of_the_Art)

## Normative and ethical debates on VAAs  

Despite their promise, VAAs raise significant normative and ethical questions. A recent assessment of representative European VAAs, using the EU’s Ethics Guidelines for Trustworthy AI, finds that while VAAs score comparably across tools, their scores are low on many requirements. The study highlights shortcomings in transparency about the subjectivity of recommendations, limited diversity of stakeholder participation, insufficient user‑centric documentation of matching algorithms, and weak disclosure of underlying values and assumptions. [pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39308758/)

These concerns are acute in European politics, where VAAs may influence participation and choice in multi‑party systems, yet operate without a clear “ground truth” for correct advice. The design of statements, choice of issues, scaling of positions, and algorithmic decision rules can all subtly shape the guidance provided to voters, raising questions about bias, neutrality, and democratic legitimacy. Consequently, VAAs are increasingly discussed not only as research instruments but as normative actors in democratic societies, requiring robust governance and transparency frameworks. [ecpr](https://ecpr.eu/group/voting-advice-applications)

## Overview of CHES  

The Chapel Hill Expert Surveys (CHES) are a long‑running series of expert surveys that estimate party positioning in Europe on multiple ideological and policy dimensions. The first CHES wave was conducted in 1999, initially covering 14 Western European countries, and subsequent waves took place in 2002, 2006, 2010, 2014, 2019, and 2024, gradually expanding to include all EU member states and additional European countries. [maxwell.syr](https://www.maxwell.syr.edu/research/article/the-2024-chapel-hill-expert-survey-on-political-party-positioning-in-europe)

By 2019, CHES provided party position data for all 28 EU member states (including the UK), plus Norway, Switzerland, and several non‑EU European states, yielding 1,196 party‑year observations in the trend file. The 2024 wave covers 279 parties in 31 countries, including all EU members except Luxembourg, plus Iceland, Norway, Switzerland, Turkey, and the UK, with 609 political scientists contributing expert judgments. When combined, CHES data now offer over 25 years of time‑series evidence on European party positioning, making it the most extensive expert survey series on European parties. [hooghe.web.unc](https://hooghe.web.unc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/11492/2021/11/2021_Jolly-et-al_-Chapel-Hill-Expert-Survey-trend-file-1999-2019.pdf)

## CHES dimensions and measures  

CHES focuses on several core dimensions of party competition. All waves include measures of parties’ general stance on European integration, a general left/right placement, and more specific economic and cultural left/right scales. The economic scale (often coded as lrecon) ranges from 0 (“extreme left”) to 10 (“extreme right”), while a cultural GAL–TAN (Green/Alternative/Libertarian vs Traditional/Authoritarian/Nationalist) dimension captures post‑materialist versus authoritarian‑nationalist orientations. [foundationsofeuropeanpolitics](https://www.foundationsofeuropeanpolitics.com/edition2/chapter9/)

Over time, CHES surveys have added items on specific EU policies (e.g., deregulation, environment, immigration, multiculturalism) and non‑EU issues such as anti‑elite rhetoric, redistribution, decentralization, and environmental policy. Recent waves also incorporate indicators of populism, democracy, and institutional dimensions like executive constraints and judicial independence, enabling analyses of horizontal accountability and democratic backsliding. This multidimensional structure makes CHES central to empirical work on European party competition and polarization. [sciencedirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0261379421001323)

## CHES findings on European politics  

Analyses of the CHES trend file show that party positions on European integration have been relatively stable from 1999 to 2019, despite major changes in EU politics, with average support rising from about 4.7 to 5.05 on a 1–7 scale. Nonetheless, CHES reveals meaningful variation: mainstream centre‑left and centre‑right parties in core member states tend to be strongly pro‑EU, while parties on the radical left and radical right are more critical of European integration. [polarization](https://polarization.wiki/data/ches/)

CHES data have been used to document the emergence of a transnational cleavage that ties attitudes toward European integration to immigration and cultural issues, particularly among radical right parties. Research using the 1999–2019 trend file finds statistically significant correlations between EU positioning and both economic left–right and GAL–TAN placements, with right‑leaning parties generally more favorable to the EU and TAN‑oriented parties more Eurosceptic. The 2024 survey note further explores potential shifts in radical right parties’ economic positioning and their relation to this transnational cleavage. [maxwell.syr](https://www.maxwell.syr.edu/research/article/the-2024-chapel-hill-expert-survey-on-political-party-positioning-in-europe)

## CHES as research infrastructure  

CHES is explicitly designed as a research infrastructure, run by a team of leading scholars and widely used in electoral studies, party research, and democratic theory. Expert judgments are typically validated against other data sources, including party manifestos (e.g., Comparative Manifesto Project) and election studies surveys, and reliability analyses suggest that experts produce consistent and coherent party placements. [chesdata](https://www.chesdata.eu/ches-europe)

Because CHES spans many countries and multiple waves, it supports sophisticated cross‑national and longitudinal analyses of European party systems: tracking shifts in economic ideology, mapping the rise of green and radical right parties, measuring elite‑level polarization, and linking party positions to mass attitudes and electoral outcomes. For studies of European integration and EU legitimacy, CHES offers one of the few systematic measures of parties’ EU positions over time, which can be combined with other datasets to explore representation and contestation in the EU polity. [foundationsofeuropeanpolitics](https://www.foundationsofeuropeanpolitics.com/edition2/chapter9/)

## Linking VAAs and CHES  

VAAs like EU&I and expert surveys like CHES are increasingly used in tandem to analyze European politics, since both generate systematic party‑position data with overlapping scopes and dimensions. The euandi trend file explicitly builds on a methodology that combines party self‑placement and expert judgment, mirroring CHES’s reliance on expert coding while embedding it into a citizen‑facing platform. [euandi](https://euandi.eu/)

Researchers have begun to cross‑validate VAA party positions against CHES estimates to assess measurement reliability and potential biases. Because CHES provides long time‑series and explicit ideological scales (e.g., lrecon and GAL–TAN), while EU&I offers election‑specific but spatially rich party placements, triangulating the two can refine our understanding of where parties stand and how they communicate their positions to different audiences. [hooghe.web.unc](https://hooghe.web.unc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/11492/2021/11/2021_Jolly-et-al_-Chapel-Hill-Expert-Survey-trend-file-1999-2019.pdf)

Moreover, CHES’s expert‑based elite positions can be compared with VAA‑based mass distributions from user responses, enabling investigations of representation gaps and the alignment or misalignment between party elites and voters across Europe. This is particularly relevant for studying the EU integration cleavage, populism, and issue competition around immigration, environment, and social policy, where both elite and mass attitudes show complex cross‑national patterns. [sciencedirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0261379421001323)

## Methodological complementarities  

Methodologically, VAAs and CHES complement each other in several ways. VAAs capture individual‑level preferences and behavioral data, often with very large samples, but their party placements are tied to specific elections and statement sets, limiting longitudinal comparisons beyond carefully designed trend files like euandi. CHES, by contrast, focuses on expert‑assessed elite positions across waves and provides standardized scales for core dimensions, making it ideal for long‑term trend analysis. [euandi](https://euandi.eu/en/about/overview.html)

VAAs rely on algorithmic matching to translate policy proximity into voting recommendations, introducing design choices whose normative implications must be scrutinized. CHES does not intervene directly in citizens’ vote choices but shapes scholarly and public understanding of party competition, with potential downstream effects on media narratives and political debates. In European politics, where both expert discourse and citizen decision‑support now rely on structured data, the interaction between VAA architectures and CHES metrics matters for how the ideological map of Europe is drawn and circulated. [polarization](https://polarization.wiki/data/ches/)

## Bias, validity, and ethics  

Both VAAs and CHES face questions of bias and validity. In VAAs, statement selection, issue framing, party inclusion rules, and algorithm design can favor certain types of parties or issue agendas, even when developers strive for neutrality and transparency. For CHES, potential biases arise from expert selection, disciplinary perspectives, and cross‑national comparability of judgments, though repeated waves and validation studies mitigate many concerns. [pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39308758/)

The recent study on VAA trustworthiness in Europe underlines that citizens’ reliance on these tools may outpace the robustness of ethical and governance frameworks, calling for improvements in transparency, stakeholder diversity, documentation, and value disclosure. Parallel debates around expert surveys stress the need to make coding choices, question wording, and aggregation procedures as transparent as possible, particularly when CHES data are used in high‑stakes analyses of democratic backsliding or populism. In the European political context—marked by multi‑level governance, rising polarization, and contested integration—these methodological and ethical issues have direct implications for how democracy is understood and practiced. [maxwell.syr](https://www.maxwell.syr.edu/research/article/the-2024-chapel-hill-expert-survey-on-political-party-positioning-in-europe)

## Significance for European politics  

Taken together, VAAs and CHES have reshaped how scholars and citizens perceive European party competition and ideological space. VAAs like EU&I give individual voters an “unobstructed view of the European political space and their place within it”, presenting EP elections as opportunities to situate oneself among hundreds of parties across the EU. CHES, meanwhile, provides the backbone for much of the empirical literature on European integration, radical right parties, new cleavages, and elite‑level polarization. [euandi](https://euandi.eu/sk/faq.html)

In contemporary European politics—characterized by Euroscepticism, populist challenges, and cross‑national ideological realignments—the combined use of VAA and CHES data enables fine‑grained analyses of how parties position themselves, how citizens perceive and choose between them, and how representation evolves over time. For legal and normative debates about the EU’s democratic deficit, these tools help illuminate both elite contestation and mass engagement, but they also raise new questions about data‑driven democracy, algorithmic mediation, and the responsibilities of academic actors in shaping electoral behavior. [academia](https://www.academia.edu/281395/Voting_Advice_Applications_in_Europe_The_State_of_the_Art)
